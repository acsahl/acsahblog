As artificial intelligence gets harder for us to control, the next question is obvious: what can we do about it? I recently watched former President Obama's conversation at Colgate University, which aired on C-SPAN. He warned about what happens when this technology ends up in the hands of "bad humans," or when models keep improving themselves until they "don't need to listen to humans anymore." His answer was blunt: "Government has to be regulating this." He pointed out that we don't leave safety to the market in other industries that can hurt people: "That's not how we treat airlines or drug companies, food companies."

We actually ran a version of this in my AI and Data Ethics class. Each team was a "company." Every round, each company picked a number from 1 to 4. The higher the number, the more market share you gained and the more risk you added. The catch is that the risk didn't land on your own company. It was a shared, global risk, far enough in the future that none of us could really see it. That is the position AI companies are in right now.

This isn't to say these companies don't care. If anything, I'd argue they care the most. OpenAI was started in part by people who didn't want to leave this technology to Google alone. Anthropic was started by people who left OpenAI wanting to be even more careful. They are the ones most aware that there is a nonzero chance this goes very badly.

Back to the game. The only way we all succeeded was by making a pact. We all agreed to play 0 for the round, and anyone who agreed and then broke the pact lost a big chunk of market share. Something similar happened in real life last month, when Anthropic's CEO proposed slowing development and the heads of OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google DeepMind publicly agreed.

The difference is that our pact had a penalty. The real one doesn't. And the pressure to break it is enormous: these companies are valued on how fast they grow, and their investors want returns quickly. That is how potentially dangerous models get released before anyone is sure they're safe. As long as money is in the picture, we can't count on morality alone.

That is why I think regulation needs to come into play now. Regulation is slow. It has to move through approvals and processes that take years, and AI companies have no such brake. It would be naive to say safety is simply each company's moral responsibility, because every company sees safety through the same filter: money. A rule that applies to everyone, with real consequences for breaking it, is the only version of our classroom pact that works outside the classroom.
